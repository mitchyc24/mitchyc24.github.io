    Background

    What a waveguide is, and why it has a cutoff

    A waveguide is a metal pipe, usually rectangular, that carries electromagnetic energy from a radio or radar to an antenna with very low loss. It has no centre conductor, so it cannot carry DC or low frequencies the way coax can.

    The walls are (nearly) perfect conductors, which forces a boundary condition: the electric field cannot run along a metal wall, only straight into it. So the field across the pipe has to fit whole numbers of half-waves between the walls, like a guitar string pinned at both ends.

    A wave only fits if half its wavelength is shorter than the pipe is wide. Below that point the pattern cannot form, the field dies away exponentially within a short distance (evanescent) and no power travels. That boundary is the cutoff frequency:

    fc = c2√εr √( (m/a)² + (n/b)² )

    For the common TE10 mode this is simply fc = c / 2a: the broad wall must be at least half a wavelength wide.

    Mode names: TE, TM, and the numbers m and n

    TE (transverse electric) modes have no electric field pointing along the guide; E lies entirely in the cross-section and there is a magnetic component Hz along the axis. TM (transverse magnetic) modes are the reverse: H is entirely in the cross-section and E has a component Ez.

    The subscripts count half-wave variations: m across the broad wall a and n across the narrow wall b. TE10 has one half-wave hump across a and no variation across b.

    TM modes need both m and n to be at least 1, because Ez is a product of sines and vanishes if either is zero. That is why there is no TM10. When both are at least 1, TEmn and TMmn share a cutoff frequency: they are degenerate.

    Why no TEM? Coax and twin-lead carry a TEM mode (no field along the axis at all) that works down to DC. A TEM wave needs two separate conductors to hold the voltage between, and a hollow pipe has only one, so every waveguide mode has a cutoff.

    The dominant mode and single-mode operation

    The mode with the lowest cutoff is the dominant mode: TE10 whenever a > b. Between its cutoff and the next one, it is the only mode that can travel, so the energy arrives in one predictable pattern. That is the range designers use.

    Standard guides make b about half of a. Then TE20 (at 2fc10) and TE01 (at about the same place) both switch on near twice the dominant cutoff, giving the widest possible single-mode band. A taller guide would let TE01 in earlier; a flatter one handles less power before the air breaks down and has more loss.

    In practice the recommended band is about 1.25 to 1.9 × fc10: close to cutoff the loss and dispersion rise steeply, and close to the next mode stray energy can leak into it at bends and joints.

    Phase velocity, group velocity and dispersion

    Inside the guide the wavelength along the axis, the guide wavelength λg, is longer than in free space:

    λg = λ / √(1 − (fc/f)²)

    The wave crests therefore sweep along faster than light: the phase velocity vp = c / √(1 − (fc/f)²) is greater than c. Nothing physical travels at that speed. Energy and information move at the group velocity vg = c √(1 − (fc/f)²), which is always slower than c, and vp · vg = c².

    Because both depend on frequency, a waveguide is dispersive: different frequencies in a pulse travel at different speeds, so short pulses spread out, especially near cutoff. The curves in the dispersion chart show this: vp is ω/β (the slope of a line from the origin to the point) and vg is dω/dβ (the slope of the curve itself).

    The bouncing plane waves view shows where this comes from. Any mode can be built from plane waves travelling at c and zig-zagging between the walls at angle θ, with sin θ = fc/f. The energy's forward progress is c cos θ (the group velocity), while the point where a crest meets the wall slides along at c / cos θ (the phase velocity). At cutoff the waves bounce straight across and make no forward progress at all.

    Waveguides and antennas

    Waveguides feed most microwave antennas: radar dishes, satellite ground stations, point-to-point links, and the feeds of radio telescopes. A few common ways a waveguide becomes an antenna:

    • Horn antenna: the guide flares out so the TE10 field spreads over a larger aperture and matches smoothly into free space. The larger the mouth, the narrower the beam.
    • Open-ended waveguide: the simplest radiator, often used as a probe for measurements.
    • Slotted waveguide array: slots cut in the wall interrupt the wall currents and each slot radiates. Spaced every half guide wavelength, they add up into a narrow fan beam. Marine and aircraft radars use them.
    • Feed horns for dishes: often circular guides, whose symmetric modes handle two polarisations at once.

    The guide size sets the band: a 3 GHz radar uses WR-284 (72 mm wide), a 10 GHz X-band system WR-90 (23 mm), and a 60 GHz link WR-15 (under 4 mm).

    Other shapes: circular guides

    Circular guides work the same way, but the patterns follow Bessel functions instead of sines. Their dominant mode is TE11, with cutoff fc = 1.841 c / (2π r). The TE01 circular mode is unusual: its loss falls as frequency rises, which once made it a candidate for long-haul millimetre-wave trunk lines before optical fibre won. An optical fibre is itself a dielectric waveguide with its own family of modes.
